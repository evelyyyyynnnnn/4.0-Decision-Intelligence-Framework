Optimization Under Uncertainty

Deterministic, sample-average, CVaR and robust formulations of the same allocation problem — fitted in-sample, judged out-of-sample, in portfolio allocation and hospital staffing.

Data

Scenarios are generated by src/problems.py, deliberately as mixtures with crash and surge regimes: on Gaussian data every method here would score identically and the comparison would be empty. Every method is fitted on one sample and scored on a different, larger one.

Four ways to be wrong about the future

All four formulations solve the same allocation problem and differ only in what they optimise against: the mean scenario, the average across sampled scenarios, the worst tail, or an adversarially chosen parameter inside an uncertainty set. Two problems, because a bad decision costs different things — money in one, unmet clinical demand in the other, and the second is not symmetric with over-provision.

Every method is fitted on one scenario sample and scored on a different, larger one. That split is the whole discipline: a stochastic program evaluated on the scenarios it optimised over reports its own objective back to itself.

The bug that looked like a finding

Pure CVaR minimisation put everything in two assets

The first version of the CVaR portfolio had no position limit. It scored worse out of sample than equal weighting, with a concentration of 1.000. The obvious diagnosis was tail overfitting — CVaR-90 estimates its objective from the worst 10% of the training sample, which at 600 scenarios is 60 points.

That diagnosis was wrong, and the sweep below is what ruled it out. If the problem were sample size, the disadvantage would shrink as the sample grew. It did not: at 6,400 scenarios the gap was unchanged and concentration was still above 0.97. The cause was the formulation. Minimising CVaR over a simplex is a linear program, and a linear program's optimum sits at a vertex, so the solver has no reason to hold more than a handful of assets.

Adding a return target did not fix it either, because the concentrated solution already had the highest expected return and the constraint never bound. What was missing is a position limit — which every real mandate carries. With one, CVaR becomes the best method on the board.

Sweep re-run with the position limit in place; CVaR now beats SAA at every sample size, by 0.068 to 0.069 CVaR.

The CVaR plan solved its objective and still lost

Read the first two numeric columns together. The CVaR plan has the best in-sample tail cost (579.9, better than both alternatives) and the worst out-of-sample tail cost (556.3). It optimised exactly what it was asked to and the answer did not transfer.

The reason is in the last metric on this page: mean pairwise demand correlation across units is 0.47. The surge is a common shock — an epidemic or a mass-casualty event hits every unit at once — and no reallocation of a fixed budget can hedge a common shock. The CVaR plan pays for tail protection it cannot deliver, and the payment shows up as a worse mean.

This is the opposite of the portfolio result and for a precise reason. There, correlation rises in the crash regime but stays below 1, so diversification still buys something. Here it does not. The lesson is not "CVaR is good" or "CVaR is bad" — it is that tail-focused allocation helps only when the tail risk is partly idiosyncratic, and the honest answer to a common shock is a bigger budget, not a cleverer split of the one you have.

Reproduce it

cd optimization-under-uncertainty
pip install -r requirements.txt
python -m pytest tests/ -q
python -m src.demo

Solvers use scipy's linprog and SLSQP so the repository has no modelling-language dependency. That caps problem size, and a production version belongs in cvxpy or Pyomo.

What this does not establish

  • Both problems are synthetic. The generators were written by the same person as the solvers, and the conclusions are about those generators.
  • The staffing result depends on how much of the surge is common across units. A hospital whose demand shocks are unit-specific would show the opposite.
  • The robust formulation uses a box uncertainty set with a closed-form inner maximisation. Ellipsoidal and budgeted sets behave differently and are not implemented.
  • No transaction costs, no rebalancing, no multi-period dynamics. Single-period allocation only.
  • Staffing treats nurses as divisible and interchangeable. Real rostering has integrality, skill mix and shift-pattern constraints.
